How the score is calculated

For each model and each 2½ minutes from 20 minutes before to 30 minutes after the event, the page computes the sun's true elevation at your location and traces a ray to two targets overhead: high cloud at 8.5 km and mid cloud at 4.5 km. Along the way it samples cloud cover at 12 points out to 450 km toward the sun.

  • The same is done for the clouds you actually look at 25 and 50 km toward the sun, not only those straight overhead, as ray-based forecasters like Sunsethue do.
  • Wherever the ray passes through a layer, light is dimmed: low cloud blocks almost fully, mid cloud strongly, high cloud only slightly. If the ray drops below the terrain, the target is in Earth's shadow.
  • The glow is the overhead canvas (how much high and mid cloud is there to catch the light, peaking around 50% each) × the light that gets through × how much of it you can see past low cloud at your spot. A solid overcast sheet is penalised.
  • Colour is weighted towards the sun sitting below about 2° and peaks once it is at or under the horizon, when the light reaching the clouds turns red. Low-level aerosol (CAMS) and near‑ground humidity can only lower the result: the NOAA Storm Prediction Center's sunset science notes that abundant haze and pollution subdue colours rather than enhance them.
  • A separate ray from your eye toward the sun about 8 minutes before it sets checks whether the sun disc itself is hidden by low cloud or hills.
  • Golden hour is the sun between 6° above and 4° below the horizon; blue hour between 4° and 6° below.
  • The score is the peak glow, 0–100. Model scores are combined with weights for resolution and model type; the range shows their disagreement.

It is a heuristic, not a physical radiative model: cloud thickness, multiple layers in one grid cell, and haze colour are not modelled.
